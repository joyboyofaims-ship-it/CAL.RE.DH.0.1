2. Concentration Calculation & Analysis

Bioassay Principles & Protocol

Guinea-Pig Ileum Assay: Histamine causes dose-dependent smooth muscle contraction via H1 receptors. Peak height (mm) and % of maximum contraction (% Emax) are read from the kymograph.

1. Matching Method:
Find a standard volume (Vs) of known concentration (As) giving the same response as the test volume (Vt).
Unknown Conc (A_t) = (A_s × V_s) / V_t

2. Interpolation Method:
Choose a lower standard (S1) and higher standard (S2) so the test response (Rt) lies between R1 and R2. Using semi-log dose-response linearity:
Log(Dose_t) = Log(Dose_1) + [(R_t - R_1)/(R_2 - R_1)] × [Log(Dose_2) - Log(Dose_1)]

Working Range: Use the near-linear middle portion of the log dose–response curve, approximately 20–80% of maximum, for interpolation. Responses outside this portion or at the 42 mm plateau are flagged.

Simulation Data & Assumptions: Responses are anchored to the ideal observations for a 10 µg/mL histamine standard: 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6 and 3.2 mL give 7, 16, 22, 34, 42 and 42 mm, respectively. Values between these doses use log-dose interpolation. Below 1 µg, the tracing uses a conservative zero-response display when the extrapolated response would be negative; this is a display convention, not a measured threshold. Generated unknown concentrations, the 1 mm matching tolerance and the 10% score threshold are practice-simulator conventions, not published laboratory acceptance criteria.

1. Why is guinea-pig ileum preferred for histamine bioassay?
Because it is highly sensitive to histamine and gives graded, reproducible H1-mediated contractions when properly maintained.
  • Very high sensitivity: it is rich in H1 receptors and contracts to histamine in the nanogram range, so even a tiny amount in a sample can be measured.
  • Graded, log-linear response: the dose-response curve is approximately straight over a useful middle range on a log-dose scale, which supports matching and interpolation assays.
  • Stable baseline: little spontaneous activity, and the tissue stays alive for hours in aerated Tyrode solution at 37 °C.
  • H1 mechanism can be tested: an H1 antagonist such as mepyramine competitively inhibits the contraction; atropine can be used to reduce muscarinic interference from acetylcholine in a sample.
  • Practical: cheap, easy to mount, and gives reproducible contractions on repeated doses (with a regular washout interval).
Fun fact: the guinea pig is famously histamine-sensitive. In guinea pigs, anaphylaxis kills mainly by severe bronchospasm, which is why they are also a classic allergy model.
2. How would a competitive antagonist affect the dose-response curve of histamine?
Parallel rightward shift of the curve, with the same maximum (Emax).
  • An H1 antagonist such as mepyramine or chlorpheniramine competes with histamine for the same receptor sites.
  • The curve shifts to the right and stays parallel, because more histamine is needed to get the same response (EC50 rises, potency of histamine falls).
  • Emax is unchanged: the block is surmountable. A large enough dose of histamine overcomes the antagonist.
  • The size of the shift is the dose ratio. The antagonist's potency is expressed as its pA2: the negative log of the molar concentration of antagonist that makes you double the agonist dose to get the same effect.

3. Why is Tyrode's solution used? What is its physiological importance?
It copies the ionic make-up of extracellular fluid, so the isolated gut stays alive and contracts normally outside the body.
  • NaCl: keeps the fluid isotonic (correct osmotic balance) and supplies Na+.
  • KCl: sets the resting membrane potential and excitability of the muscle.
  • CaCl2: essential for excitation–contraction coupling. Without Ca2+ smooth muscle will not contract to histamine.
  • MgCl2 and NaH2PO4: support membrane stability and help buffering.
  • NaHCO3: bicarbonate buffer to hold the pH near 7.4.
  • Glucose: energy source for the tissue.

It is kept at 37 °C and bubbled with air or oxygen so the tissue gets oxygen for ATP and stays at body conditions.

Remember: Ringer is the classic solution for frog (amphibian) tissue, while Tyrode or Krebs is used for mammalian tissue such as guinea-pig ileum.
4. What are the physiological effects of histamine in the human body?
Histamine acts through four receptor types (H1 to H4). Think: vessels, smooth muscle, stomach, heart, brain.
ReceptorMain effects
H1Contracts bronchial and intestinal smooth muscle (bronchospasm, colic). Dilates small vessels through endothelial NO and raises capillary permeability (fall in BP, oedema, wheal). Stimulates sensory nerve endings (itch, pain). Promotes wakefulness in the brain.
H2Gastric acid secretion from parietal cells (its most important physiological role). Cardiac stimulation: increased rate and force. Slower, sustained vasodilation.
H3Presynaptic autoreceptor, mainly in the brain: reduces release of histamine and other transmitters.
H4On immune cells (eosinophils, mast cells): chemotaxis and a role in inflammation and itch.
  • Triple response of Lewis (intradermal histamine): red spot (capillary dilation), flare (axon-reflex arteriolar dilation) and wheal (increased permeability).
  • Brain: a neurotransmitter for wakefulness, appetite control and the vestibular system. This is why first-generation antihistamines cause drowsiness.
  • Large doses also release adrenaline from the adrenal medulla.
  • Stored mainly in mast cells and basophils, in gastric ECL cells and in neurons.
The name comes from the Greek histos (tissue). Dale and Laidlaw described its powerful actions in 1910, and it was later isolated from liver and lung tissue.
5. Name clinical conditions where histamine plays a major role.
Hives, nose, wheeze, acid and flush: allergy and stomach acid are the big two.
  • Allergic conditions: urticaria, allergic rhinitis (hay fever), angioedema, allergic conjunctivitis, pruritus, reactions to insect stings. Treated with H1 antihistamines.
  • Anaphylactic shock (type I hypersensitivity): mast-cell degranulation causes sudden vasodilation, hypotension and bronchospasm. Adrenaline is the life-saving drug (it is the physiological antagonist).
  • Bronchial asthma: histamine contributes to bronchoconstriction, but H1 blockers are only of limited use because other mediators matter more.
  • Peptic ulcer, GERD and Zollinger–Ellison syndrome: excess acid, treated with H2 blockers (for example famotidine, cimetidine).
  • Systemic mastocytosis and some carcinoid or gastric tumours: excess histamine causes flushing, itching and diarrhoea.
  • Scombroid fish poisoning: spoiled tuna or mackerel contains a lot of histamine, causing flushing, headache and rash soon after eating.
  • Drug-induced histamine release: morphine, d-tubocurarine and vancomycin (red man syndrome) can cause flushing and hypotension without a true allergy.
  • Motion sickness and vertigo: H1 antagonists such as promethazine and cinnarizine help.
