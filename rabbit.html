Viva questions

Q1–Q5: control eye, nerve supply, light, accommodation and corneal reflexes
Q1. Why is one eye used as a control?

One eye gets the test drug, the other gets normal saline. Comparing the two shows which changes are caused by the drug.

Key points

  • Same animal, same light, same conditions, so no animal-to-animal variation.
  • Rules out the effects of handling, the drop itself and the procedure.
  • Saline (0.9%, isotonic) is inert: no effect on pupil, reflexes or IOP.
  • Control values: pupil 6 mm, light and corneal reflexes present, IOP about 15 mmHg.
Viva line: One eye is the control so that the drug effect can be separated from handling and instillation effects, in the same animal under the same conditions.
Q2. What is the nerve supply of the iris and ciliary muscle?
StructureNerve supplyReceptorAction
Dilator pupillae (radial)Sympathetic onlyAlpha-1Contracts: mydriasis
Sphincter pupillae (circular)Parasympathetic onlyM3Contracts: miosis
Ciliary muscleParasympathetic onlyM3Contracts: accommodation (near vision)

Pathways

  • Parasympathetic: Edinger-Westphal nucleus → oculomotor nerve (III) → ciliary ganglion (relay) → short ciliary nerves.
  • Sympathetic: descending fibres → C8–T2 (intermediolateral column) → superior cervical ganglion → along the internal carotid artery → long ciliary nerves.
  • The ciliary ganglion has three roots (parasympathetic, sympathetic, sensory). Only the parasympathetic fibres synapse there.
  • Sensation from the iris and cornea: ophthalmic nerve (V1).

Link to drugs

  • Every pupil drug acts on alpha-1 receptors (radial muscle) or M3 receptors (sphincter and ciliary muscle). This decides pupil size.
  • Atropine blocks M3 receptors on the sphincter and ciliary muscle: mydriasis and cycloplegia.
  • Atropine also reduces tears, because the lacrimal gland is supplied by parasympathetic fibres (superior salivatory nucleus → pterygopalatine ganglion).

Viva line: The radial muscle is sympathetic (alpha-1); the circular muscle and the ciliary muscle are parasympathetic (M3).
Q3. What is the light reflex pathway?
LimbPath
AfferentRetina → optic nerve (II) → optic chiasma → optic tract → pretectal nucleus
CentrePretectal nucleus → Edinger-Westphal nuclei of both sides
EfferentEdinger-Westphal nucleus → oculomotor nerve (III) → ciliary ganglion → short ciliary nerves → sphincter pupillae (ACh on M3) → constriction

Key points

  • Direct response: the lit eye constricts. Consensual response: the other eye constricts too (the crossing at the Edinger-Westphal nuclei).
  • The sympathetic system has only a tonic role in the light reflex.
  • In the simulator the reflex is lost only with atropine: the pathway is intact, but the M3 receptors on the sphincter are blocked. Phenylephrine, ephedrine and adrenaline keep the reflex.
  • The pupillary light reflex is widely used to detect autonomic dysfunction.
Viva line: Light reflex: afferent optic nerve (II), efferent oculomotor nerve (III), parasympathetic.
Q4. What is the accommodation reflex pathway?

Also called the near reflex: the response to focusing on a near object.

Pathway

  • Afferent: retina → optic nerve (II) → visual cortex. The cortex is involved, unlike in the light reflex.
  • Efferent: via the midbrain → Edinger-Westphal nucleus and oculomotor nerve (III) → ciliary ganglion.

Three responses (triad)

  • Ciliary muscle contracts (M3) → zonules slacken → lens becomes more convex.
  • Sphincter pupillae contracts → pupil constricts (sharper image).
  • Medial recti contract → convergence of both eyes.

Link to drugs

  • Atropine: paralysis of accommodation (cycloplegia).
  • Physostigmine and pilocarpine: contraction of the ciliary muscle (spasm of accommodation).
  • Difference from the light reflex: the light reflex needs no cortex. In Argyll Robertson pupil, accommodation is present but the light reflex is lost.
Viva line: Near reflex: ciliary muscle contraction, miosis and convergence.
Q5. What is the corneal reflex pathway?

Touching the cornea with cotton causes a blink.

LimbPath
AfferentCornea → ophthalmic nerve (V1) → trigeminal ganglion → spinal trigeminal nucleus
CentreInterneurons → facial nucleus (both sides)
EfferentFacial nerve (VII) → orbicularis oculi → blink

Key points

  • Both eyes blink (direct and consensual) because the connections reach the facial nuclei of both sides.
  • Parasympathetic fibres of VII also supply the lacrimal gland: reflex tearing.
  • In the simulator the reflex is lost only with lignocaine. It blocks Na⁺ channels in the sensory (V1) endings of the cornea, so the afferent limb fails. The pupil is unchanged.
Viva line: Corneal reflex: afferent trigeminal (V1), efferent facial (VII).

Observation: The pupil of the test eye increases from 6 mm to 10 mm (mydriasis). Light reflex and corneal reflex are intact.

Inference: The given drug is an adrenergic agonist.

1. Mechanism of action
  • Phenylephrine: non-catecholamine, directly acting, selective α1 agonist.
  • Ephedrine: non-catecholamine, mixed action. Mainly releases noradrenaline from nerve endings (indirect), plus some direct α and β action. Can cause tachyphylaxis.
  • Adrenaline: catecholamine, direct agonist at α and β receptors.
2. Effect on pupil, light reflex and corneal reflex
  • They activate α1 receptors on the radial (dilator) muscle. The muscle contracts and the pupil widens: active mydriasis.
  • The ciliary muscle is not affected, so no cycloplegia and accommodation is preserved.
  • Light reflex intact: the path is retina → optic nerve → midbrain → 3rd nerve → sphincter pupillae (M3). Adrenergic drugs act only on the radial muscle and do not block it. The response may look slightly weaker because the dilated radial muscle opposes it.
  • Corneal reflex intact: it uses the 5th nerve (sensory) and 7th nerve (blink). Adrenergic drugs affect neither and have no local anaesthetic action.
3. Clinical uses and rationale
  • Phenylephrine (2.5–10%): fundus examination (short-acting mydriasis without cycloplegia); combined with a mild antimuscarinic for refraction; in iridocyclitis to break synechiae; decongestant. Caution: 10% solution can cause systemic hypertension. Pseudoephedrine is used for nasal decongestion (tachyphylaxis can occur).
  • Ephedrine: rarely used in the eye now. Main uses: orthostatic hypotension and hypotension (e.g. during spinal anaesthesia), nasal decongestion. Tachyphylaxis because it depletes noradrenaline stores.
  • Adrenaline: poor topical use because it penetrates the cornea badly. Used intracamerally in cataract surgery to maintain mydriasis; its prodrug dipivefrin is used in open-angle glaucoma to lower IOP. Also used in anaphylactic shock and ACLS.

Inference: The given drug is a cholinergic antagonist (antimuscarinic).

Effects of atropine and other antimuscarinics on the eye

  • Passive mydriasis
  • Paralysis of accommodation (cycloplegia)
  • Rise in intraocular pressure
  • Decrease in lacrimation
1. Mechanism of action
  • Atropine is a competitive antagonist of muscarinic receptors (ACh is blocked from M3 receptors in the iris and ciliary muscle).
  • Tropicamide and homatropine act in the same way but are shorter acting.
2. Effect on pupil, light reflex and corneal reflex
  • They block M3 receptors on the sphincter pupillae. It relaxes and the unopposed radial muscle widens the pupil: passive mydriasis.
  • They block M3 receptors on the ciliary muscle. It relaxes, causing loss of accommodation (cycloplegia).
  • Light reflex lost: the reflex ends on the sphincter pupillae through M3 receptors. When these are blocked, the muscle cannot contract even though light is detected.
  • Corneal reflex intact: it uses the 5th nerve (sensory) and 7th nerve (blink), which are not muscarinic pathways, and atropine has no local anaesthetic action.
3. Clinical uses and rationale
  • Tropicamide: refractive error testing and fundus examination. Short duration of mydriasis and cycloplegia, so the patient recovers quickly.
  • Homatropine: iridocyclitis, used alternately with a miotic. Alternating mydriasis and miosis prevents adhesions (synechiae) between iris and lens.
  • Atropine: antidote in organophosphorus (OPC) poisoning. Blocks muscarinic effects of excess ACh (salivation, bronchorrhoea, bradycardia, miosis); given in repeated large doses until atropinisation.
4. Contraindication for local use in the eye
  • Narrow-angle glaucoma. Mydriasis bunches the iris at the periphery and narrows the irido-corneal angle. This blocks drainage of aqueous humour through the canal of Schlemm and can precipitate acute congestive glaucoma (a rise in IOP).

Observation: No change in pupillary diameter in the test eye (7 mm throughout). Light reflex intact. Corneal reflex lost from 2 min onwards.

Inference: The given drug is a local anaesthetic.

1. Mechanism of action
  • Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels in the nerve membrane.
  • They reduce membrane permeability to Na⁺ during depolarisation, so the action potential cannot be generated or propagated and conduction is blocked.
  • They act on the sensory nerve endings of the cornea (ophthalmic branch of the 5th nerve), giving corneal anaesthesia.
Why the pupil and reflexes behave this way
  • Pupil: no change. Pupil size depends on autonomic receptors (α1 on radial muscle, M3 on sphincter). Local anaesthetics do not act on them.
  • Light reflex: intact. Retina → optic nerve → midbrain → 3rd nerve → sphincter pupillae. None of it is blocked by a topical anaesthetic.
  • Corneal reflex: lost. Its afferent limb is the sensory 5th nerve from the cornea. This is blocked, so the touch is not sensed and the blink (7th nerve) is not triggered, even though the motor limb is normal.

2. Local anaesthetic with mydriatic property
  • Cocaine is an ester-linked, intermediate-acting local anaesthetic.
  • It produces mydriasis by inhibiting neuronal uptake of noradrenaline and adrenaline (uptake-1 inhibition). More transmitter stays in the synapse and acts on α1 receptors of the radial muscle.
  • Because of this sympathomimetic action, the pupil dilates with an intact light reflex, unlike other local anaesthetics.
3. Why procaine does not produce corneal anaesthesia
  • Procaine is poorly absorbed from mucous membranes, so it does not penetrate the cornea well enough to block the sensory nerve endings.
  • It is therefore not used topically in the eye; it is used for infiltration and nerve block. Surface agents such as proparacaine, tetracaine and lignocaine are used on the cornea.
Viva line: Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. In the rabbit eye the pupil does not change and the light reflex is intact, but the corneal reflex is lost because the sensory limb is blocked. Cocaine is the exception because it also dilates the pupil (uptake-1 inhibition).
