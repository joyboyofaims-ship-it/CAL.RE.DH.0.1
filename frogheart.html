Discussion questions with model answers

Read the tracing first, then open each answer.

  1. a)Define tone. How will you measure tone, force of contraction, and heart rate?

    Tone is the residual tension in the relaxed muscle: the continuous, passive partial contraction of muscle during the resting state.

    • Tone is measured by the shift of the midline of the graph.
    • Force of contraction is measured by the increase in height of the graph.
    • Heart rate is measured by the frequency of cycles per second in the recording.
  2. b)What are the effects of epinephrine on heart rate, tone, and force? Explain the mechanism.

    Epinephrine increases heart rate (60→68 bpm), force of contraction and tone. It stimulates β₁-adrenergic receptors in the heart, raising cAMP, which causes:

    1. Increased slope of slow diastolic depolarization in the SA node: tachycardia (positive chronotropic).
    2. Acceleration of the rate of conduction (positive dromotropic).
    3. Increased cytosolic Ca²⁺ load (increase in tone).
    4. Increased opening of Ca²⁺ channels, raising force of contraction (positive inotropic).
    5. Increased rate of Ca²⁺ reuptake into the SR (positive lusitropic).
  3. c)How do propranolol and acetylcholine decrease heart rate, tone, and force?

    Propranolol decreases heart rate, tone and force of contraction by blocking β₁-adrenergic receptors, which lowers intracellular cAMP.

    Acetylcholine decreases heart rate, tone and force of contraction by stimulating the M₂ receptor and opening K⁺ channels, which hyperpolarizes myocardial cells.

    With ACh, the usual 2 mcg dose only depresses the heart. Arrest needs a higher dose; no dose is recorded, so it is a separate option marked ASSUMPTION.

  4. d)Why can calcium cause systolic arrest and potassium diastolic arrest? Give the clinical application.

    Calcium (systolic arrest): the amount of Ca²⁺ in the T-tubules is directly proportional to its concentration in the extracellular fluid. The Ca²⁺ available for contraction therefore depends directly on extracellular Ca²⁺, and a large amount given rapidly causes systolic arrest.

    Potassium (diastolic arrest): elevated extracellular K⁺ (10–40 mM) alters the myocyte resting potential from −85 mV to between −65 mV and −40 mV, leading to inactivation of fast Na⁺ channels. The new resting potential blocks conduction of the myocardial action potential, causing diastolic arrest.

    Clinical application: cardioplegic solution.

  5. e)Why may atropine cause only an insignificant heart-rate increase in an isolated frog heart?

    The isolated frog heart has no vagus nerve, so there is no baseline inhibitory vagal tone. In vivo, atropine raises heart rate by abolishing vagal tone; this mechanism fails in an isolated heart, so atropine produces only a modest rise (58→62 bpm).

  6. f)Chart-reading question: reconstruct the drug sequence and evaluate the tracing.

    Tasks: list the drug order, compare each HR with its preceding BHR, identify which drug produced the largest rate increase, and name at least two results that differ from the observation table.

    Model answer: Epi → NEpi → Iso → CaCl₂ → Prop → ACh → KCl → Atr. The chart reads: 69→76, 69→67, 69→94, 69→1, 69→73, 68→62, 68→1, and 69→91 bpm respectively. Isoprenaline produces the largest labelled rise (+25 bpm). Notable discrepancies include propranolol (69→73 in the chart versus 60→44 in the observation table) and atropine (69→91 versus 58→62). “HR 1” accompanies arrest in this teaching material and must not be interpreted as a sustainable normal heart rate.
